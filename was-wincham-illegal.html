Spain — Violations of Spanish Law

Spanish Securities Market Law — Anti-Avoidance
Article 108 LMV — The Share-for-Property Avoidance Scheme
🔴 Severity: HIGH

This is the single most important legal issue in this entire case — and it is a law that exists specifically because of arrangements like the one Wincham set up for Philip.


What the law says: Spain enacted Article 108, Ley 24/1988 del Mercado de Valores (now consolidated into the consolidated Securities Market Law) as a direct anti-avoidance provision. When a company’s assets consist primarily of Spanish real estate and someone acquires the shares in that company, Spanish tax law can pierce the corporate veil and treat the share purchase as a direct property transfer — levying ITP (Impuesto sobre Transmisiones Patrimoniales) as if the land itself had changed hands.


What actually happened: In June 2019, Philip did not buy a Spanish villa. He bought 100% of the shares in Los Romeros Limited — a UK company whose sole asset was a Spanish villa in Lanzarote. The entire commercial rationale, as Wincham explicitly stated, was to avoid paying Spanish ITP on the acquisition. This is the textbook definition of the scheme Article 108 was enacted to prevent.


The exposure Wincham created but never disclosed: AEAT (Spain’s tax authority) has the power to retrospectively assess Philip for the Canary Islands ITP of 6.5% on €185,000 = €12,025. The statute of limitations in Spain is 4 years from the date of the act. That window has likely now closed for the 2019 transaction — but it was a live, undisclosed liability for the entire duration of Philip’s ownership. No professional who recommended this structure should have done so without a clear written warning about Article 108 risk.

Legal Consequences & Implications for the Negligence Claim
  • Wincham failed to disclose a material statutory risk that arose directly from the structure they recommended
  • This risk alone is sufficient to establish breach of duty of care under Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465
  • The fact that AEAT did not ultimately pursue Philip does not diminish Wincham’s negligence in creating and concealing the exposure
  • A competent Spanish asesor fiscal would have advised on this risk as a matter of course before any ITP avoidance strategy
Spanish IRNR Compliance — Imputación de Rentas
Annual Modelo 210 Filing Failures (Potentially 15 Years)
🔴 Severity: HIGH

Non-resident companies that own Spanish property which is not being rented out are legally required to file an annual Modelo 210 (Imputación de rentas inmobiliarias) — a notional income charge based on 1.1%–2% of the cadastral value, taxed at 24% for non-EU entities (which includes UK companies post-Brexit).


The risk: Los Romeros Limited held the property from 2011. Philip took ownership in 2019. Your father did not rent the property. There is a strong probability that Wincham never advised on or filed these annual Spanish returns for any year between acquisition and the 2026 sale — a potential 15-year compliance gap.


Why this matters right now: The Spanish notary completing the March 2026 sale should have required a certificado de no deudas con Hacienda (no-debt certificate from AEAT) before signing. If the notary did not obtain this, or if debts exist that were not paid off at completion, Philip may have outstanding Spanish tax liabilities that survived the sale.

United Kingdom — Civil, Regulatory & Criminal Violations

UK Civil Law — Tort of Negligence
Negligent Misstatement — The "16% Saving" Claim
🔴 Severity: HIGH — Core Claim

Wincham made a specific, quantified financial claim to Philip: that by acquiring the company shell rather than the property directly, he would save approximately 16% in Spanish acquisition costs. This is a statement of fact upon which Philip relied to his direct financial detriment.


The law: Under the tort of negligent misstatement, established in Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465 and developed through Caparo Industries plc v Dickman [1990] UKHL 2, a professional adviser who makes a representation they know or should know will be relied upon owes a duty to ensure that representation is accurate and complete.


The four elements of the claim are all satisfied:


✅ Duty of Care: Established by the adviser-client relationship between Wincham and Philip
✅ Breach: The "16% saving" was a material overstatement (Canary Islands ITP is 6.5%, not 16%), and Wincham failed to disclose the far greater exit tax costs
✅ Causation: Philip would not have used the company structure "but for" Wincham’s advice
✅ Loss: Quantified at £49,392 net financial harm (see the companion comparison report)


Statute: Claim must be brought within 6 years of the breach or 3 years from the “date of knowledge” under Limitation Act 1980, s.14A. Given Philip is only now discovering the true financial harm, the 3-year date of knowledge clock has very likely not yet started to run.

Recovery Potential

  • Additional quantum: Wasted corporate administration fees paid to Wincham/Adrem over the holding period
  • Further quantum: Any Spanish Modelo 210 debts, penalties or surcharges caused by Wincham’s compliance failures
  • Claim governed by the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982, s.13 (implied term to carry out service with reasonable care and skill)
UK Criminal / Regulatory Law
Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 — Misleading Practices
😖 Potentially Criminal

The Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (implementing EU Directive 2005/29/EC, retained in UK law post-Brexit) prohibit misleading commercial practices by businesses supplying services to consumers.


Regulation 5 — Misleading Actions: A commercial practice is misleading if it contains false information or presents information in a way that deceives the average consumer and causes them to take a transactional decision they would not otherwise have taken. Wincham’s "save 16%" claim, if based on exaggerated or false figures, satisfies this test.


Regulation 6 — Misleading Omissions: A commercial practice is misleading if it omits material information that the average consumer needs to make an informed decision. Wincham’s failure to disclose the exit tax costs (Corporation Tax top-up, MVL costs, and personal CGT on distribution) is a textbook Regulation 6 violation.


When does it become criminal? Under Regulation 9, a trader who knowingly or recklessly engages in a misleading commercial practice commits a criminal offence. If Wincham marketed this company structure to multiple clients with the same "save 16%" pitch, the systematic nature of the conduct supports an inference of recklessness or knowledge. Maximum penalty: 2 years imprisonment and/or an unlimited fine.


Who investigates: The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) and local Trading Standards have enforcement powers. A formal complaint to either body costs Philip nothing and could trigger an investigation affecting all Wincham clients who received similar advice.

Strategic Value
  • A CMA complaint runs in parallel with the civil claim and costs nothing to file
  • A regulatory investigation dramatically weakens Wincham’s bargaining position in any settlement negotiation
  • Evidence of other affected clients discovered during an investigation strengthens Philip’s individual claim
  • Even if no criminal prosecution results, the investigation itself creates significant reputational pressure on Wincham
UK Tax Law — Criminal Offence
DOTAS Non-Disclosure — Finance Act 2004, Part 7
😖 Potentially Criminal (if multi-client scheme)

HMRC’s Disclosure of Tax Avoidance Schemes (DOTAS) regime requires promoters of certain tax avoidance arrangements to notify HMRC of the scheme and obtain a Scheme Reference Number (SRN) before marketing it to clients.


The issue: The Wincham structure — using a UK company shell to hold Spanish real estate in order to avoid Spanish acquisition taxes — has the hallmarks of a marketed tax avoidance scheme. If Wincham promoted this arrangement to more than one client (which is highly likely for a corporate services firm that specialised in Spanish property), they may have been required to disclose it to HMRC under Finance Act 2004, Part 7.


The offence: Failure by a promoter to disclose a notifiable arrangement is a criminal offence punishable by FA 2004, s.98C with an initial penalty of £600 per day rising to £1 million for continued non-disclosure, plus potential criminal prosecution for deliberate concealment.


How to trigger HMRC scrutiny: Philip can report the suspected scheme directly to HMRC’s Fraud Hotline. HMRC has strong incentives to investigate as any successful scheme notification allows them to pursue all participants for unpaid tax.

Strategic Implications
  • If HMRC opens an investigation, Wincham’s legal position deteriorates significantly across all client relationships
  • HMRC’s investigation may uncover additional recoverable losses for Philip
  • A confirmed DOTAS breach is a separate ground of civil claim against Wincham for the losses caused
